# Playbook: rules distilled from the references

Last updated 2026-11-11, from refs 1–8. Each rule names the refs behind it (`videos/`, and `brand/identity.md` §1 for refs 1–3). Where a rule conflicts with `brand/identity.md` §5–6, that file wins until the user approves the change (see `proposals-2026-11-11.md`).

**Limit of this evidence:** eight videos, watched without audio. Likes and comments were read on 2026-11-11. These are patterns that repeat across successful videos, not proven causes.

## 1. The first five seconds
1. **Line 1 is in-world dialogue, not an introduction.** A character says the problem to the other character, by name. No logo, no title, no "today we…" (refs 5, 6, 7, 8).
2. **A concrete number or stake inside the first line or the first panel:** an amount, a count, "every user" (refs 6, 7, 8).
3. **The cover is two or three words** from that first line (refs 3, 6, 7).
4. The narrated alternative is **second person**: "you are…" from the first word (ref 4).

## 2. Characters
1. **A flawed, likeable human drives.** Panicking, lazy, overconfident or shameless, never stupid. The flaw causes the incident (refs 5, 6, 7, 8).
2. **A calm expert answers** with dry humour and a light roast. It also admits its own part of the blame (refs 6, 7).
3. **The human's questions are the script's skeleton.** Short ones: "with what?", "and?", "how common is this?", "why again?". Each is the viewer's next question (refs 6, 7).
4. **Nobody speaks more than two lines in a row.** Then the other one reacts, asks or interrupts (refs 5–8).
5. **The small character sits at the human's eye level:** on a book stack, a shoulder, or floating. Both fit a close-up and the eyelines meet (refs 5, 6, 8).
6. **A third character can be off-screen:** a client or boss who exists only as notifications (ref 5).
7. **Simple faces, big reads.** Brows and eyelids do the acting; small emotes (a sweat drop, a blush) mark the beat (refs 1, 5, 6).

## 3. Dialogue
1. **Lines are short:** about 12 words at most, 2–3 seconds each. A 60 s video has 24–28 lines (refs 5, 6, 7).
2. **The first line may be held longer** (~4 s) so the premise lands (ref 5).
3. **One-word lines are the best lines:** "Done.", a corrected number, a single category (refs 5, 6, 8).
4. **Blame, roast and confession** carry the facts. A fact said as a comeback is remembered; a fact said as a lecture is skipped (refs 6, 7, 8).
5. **Rule of three** for a gag: same beat three times, faster, then break it (ref 5).

## 4. Structure
Two shapes cover all five new references.

**A. Incident → fix → tool (refs 6, 7; also 5):**

| Share of runtime | Beat |
|---|---|
| 0–8% | The disaster, with a number |
| 8–30% | The cause, shown on one panel; blame |
| 30–45% | The immediate fix |
| **45–57%** | **The reveal:** the tool or the key fact, in one line ("X open-sourced Y. Free.") |
| 57–85% | What it does (one sentence, three items) → one statistic with its sample → the result |
| 85–100% | The honest caveat as a line, then a punchline or a silent reaction |

**B. Escalation loop (ref 4; ref 8 is the step-by-step version):**
"You are X" → problem → visible fix → "But…" → next problem, every ~8 s; a surprising fact every second loop; a final test that proves the result.

Shared rules:
1. **One idea or one tool per video.**
2. **Show first, name later.** The viewer sees the thing work before learning what it's called (ref 5).
3. **The limit is a plot beat, not a disclaimer:** a refusal (ref 8), "one run finds half" (ref 7). It can be the ending.
4. **Length: 56–69 s** for dialogue pieces.
5. **A series is a template.** Same set, same characters, same shots; only the incident changes (refs 6 and 7 are near-identical in form).

## 5. Shots and editing
1. **Three shots do the work:** close-up of A, close-up of B, an insert (panel or object). Add one two-shot (refs 6, 7).
2. **Cut on every line,** every 2–3 s. Cameras are locked, with a slight push-in at most (refs 1, 6, 7).
3. **The wide shot is rationed.** One at the start or, better, one at the very end as a full stop (ref 7).
4. **One cosy night set, reused in every episode:** one warm lamp against a cool room, bokeh background (refs 1, 5, 6, 7).
5. **Split screen for step-by-step:** demo on top (~55%), characters below, still reacting (ref 8).

## 6. Panels and on-screen information
1. **One idea per panel, 3–4 s:** a counter running up, a code file with one line highlighted, a typed command with a tick, a results table (refs 6, 7).
2. **Colour by meaning:** danger, done, neutral (refs 1, 6, 7).
3. **A step counter with progress segments** tells the viewer how much is left (refs 2, 8).
4. **Labels only on the facts worth remembering,** three or four per video (ref 4).
5. **Our difference (hard rule 4):** the references invent their screens. Ours are either a **real capture** or a **schematic labelled as one**. Every number has a source in the script.

## 7. Captions
1. **Dialogue:** a full sentence per caption, with the speaker shown by colour or a label (refs 5, 8).
2. **Narration:** word by word (ref 4).
3. Captions sit where the faces aren't: top (ref 5), mid-frame over a close-up's chest (refs 6, 7), or the seam of a split screen (ref 8).

## 8. Endings and calls to action
1. **End on a callback, a twist or silence.** No "thanks for watching" (refs 5, 6, 7).
2. **The twist is about the character,** not the product (ref 5).
3. **The CTA lives outside the story:** in the post caption ("comment WORD" plus a question people want to answer about themselves), or on a 3 s end card (refs 6, 7, 8).
4. **A share prompt names a person:** "send this to the friend who…" (ref 8). That video has 5–10× the comments of the others.
5. Keyword-comment CTAs promise a link or a guide by DM. Doing that automatically needs a third-party service; for us that's a proposal, not a default.

## 9. What makes it feel interactive
1. The viewer is **addressed** ("you") or **represented** (the human asks their questions).
2. The viewer can **predict**: a gag set up three times, a step counter, a question left hanging for one beat before the answer.
3. The viewer is **asked something** they can answer in a comment.
4. The viewer can **do it**: one command, one setting, one check, shown on screen.

## 10. Checklist for a script
- [ ] Line 1: a problem, said by one character to the other, with a number or a stake.
- [ ] No line over ~12 words; nobody speaks more than two lines in a row.
- [ ] The human asks at least four short questions.
- [ ] One idea. The reveal sits between 45% and 57%.
- [ ] One statistic at most, with its source in the script.
- [ ] The limit or caveat is spoken as dialogue.
- [ ] The last beat is a callback, a twist or a silent reaction.
- [ ] Every panel is marked "real capture" or "schematic" in the storyboard.
- [ ] 55–70 s. Works with the sound off.
- [ ] Nothing in it recreates a reference's characters, set, lines or story.
